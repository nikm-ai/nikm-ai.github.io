Problem statement
Predicting 90-Day Churn
from Behavioral Engagement Signals

A tutoring platform was experiencing elevated 90-day churn rates with no systematic early-warning capability. Support teams operated reactively, contacting students only after cancellation intent had been expressed. The objective was to develop a predictive model capable of surfacing at-risk students during the engagement window when intervention remains viable, and to rank-order the at-risk population to allow targeted outreach prioritization under limited staff capacity.

Feature engineering
Behavioral Signal Construction

All features were derived from raw session and assessment event logs. No demographic or subscription-tier information was included in the final model, limiting potential for proxy discrimination. The feature set captures engagement trajectory, not just current state.

Model development
XGBoost Training and Threshold Optimization

XGBoost was selected over logistic regression and random forest after 5-fold cross-validation. The primary evaluation metric was recall rather than accuracy, given that the cost of missing an at-risk student (false negative) substantially exceeds the cost of an unnecessary outreach contact (false positive) under the client's operational model.

Classification threshold was tuned on the validation set by plotting the precision-recall curve and selecting the operating point consistent with the outreach team's capacity to contact approximately 15% of the active student population per week.

Deployment architecture
Operationalizing the
Outreach Prioritization Pipeline

The model was deployed as a weekly batch scoring job rather than a real-time inference endpoint, consistent with the support team's Monday-morning triage workflow. Scored students are ranked by predicted churn probability and surfaced in a prioritized outreach queue with the top three contributing features displayed per student.

  • Weekly batch inference on all active students with tenure under 12 months
  • Top 15% of scored population flagged for high-priority outreach
  • Students in months 1 to 3 receive a separate cohort flag given elevated baseline hazard
  • Model retrained quarterly on rolling 18-month window as engagement patterns evolve
Deployment reduced time-to-contact for at-risk students from 14 days (reactive) to 4 days (proactive), within the first quarter of operation.
The 81% recall threshold was calibrated to match the outreach team's weekly contact capacity. Operating at higher thresholds recovered additional at-risk students but exceeded staffing limits; this is documented as a scaling constraint for future investment decisions.

Context
Consolidating Fragmented Data
into Tested, Version-Controlled Layers

Three separate client engagements presented variants of the same underlying problem: operational data was distributed across incompatible systems, transformation logic lived in ad-hoc SQL scripts or spreadsheet formulas with no version control or automated testing, and downstream modeling and reporting consumed data from these untested sources.

In each case, a dbt Core project was introduced to consolidate sources, formalize transformation logic across clearly separated layers, and provide a testing and documentation framework that transferred to the client's internal team at the conclusion of the engagement.

Source integration
Salesforce, Postgres, and
Google Sheets as Unified Sources

Each source presented distinct reliability and schema challenges. Salesforce objects arrived via Fivetran with non-deterministic sync timing and occasional duplicate rows from incremental syncs. Postgres transactional tables were structurally clean but required significant type coercion. Google Sheets, used by operations teams for manual territory mappings and targets, required a source freshness SLA configured at the source declaration level to flag stale manual updates before they propagated downstream.

  • Salesforce: deduplication via row_number() over (partition by id order by _fivetran_synced desc) applied at every staging model
  • Postgres: cast applied uniformly at staging; no business logic introduced until intermediate layer
  • Google Sheets: freshness block with 24-hour warn threshold; staleness breaks the DAG before downstream models run
  • All source declarations in a single sources.yml with full column-level documentation

Every mart model shipped with a schema.yml test suite covering structural integrity and business logic assertions. Generic tests (unique, not_null, accepted_values) handled structural coverage; singular tests written in SQL caught domain-specific violations that generic tests could not express.

Test coverage was a contractual deliverable in two of the three engagements. Clients retained the test suite after handoff as a regression harness for future pipeline modifications.

In all three engagements, the primary downstream consumer of the mart layer was a Looker instance. The mart models were designed with Looker dimension and measure naming conventions in mind, reducing the volume of LookML that needed to be written against the marts. All column names, grain documentation, and test coverage were preserved in the dbt project for the client's internal data team.

Problem statement
Systematic Mispricing in
Residential Portfolio Management

Manual rent-setting at scale produces predictable inefficiencies: a portion of the portfolio is priced above market equilibrium (generating vacancy drag), while another portion is priced below (forgoing attainable revenue). Neither condition is immediately observable without a market-clearing reference price against which to benchmark current listings.

The engagement objective was to train a model on observable property and market features capable of predicting market-clearing monthly rent, then use deviations from model estimates as actionable mispricing signals surfaced to non-technical property managers through a Looker dashboard.

Mispricing detection
Operationalizing Model Estimates
as Actionable Pricing Signals

A listing was classified as materially mispriced if its current asking rent deviated from the model estimate by more than 5% in either direction. The 5% threshold was calibrated against the model's MAE: at $164/month MAE, flagging deviations smaller than approximately $110 on a $2,200 listing would produce excessive false positives from model noise rather than true pricing gaps.

  • Overpriced (above threshold): flag for rent reduction at next renewal opportunity
  • Underpriced (below threshold): flag for rent increase; demand score considered before recommendation
  • Within band: no action recommended; monitor quarterly

The high mispricing rate (72%) reflects the synthetic dataset construction and should not be interpreted as a realistic portfolio mispricing base rate. In production deployments, mispricing rates of 20 to 30% are more typical for portfolios without systematic pricing tools.
Dashboard and handoff
Looker Dashboard for
Non-Technical Property Managers

The Looker dashboard was designed specifically for property managers with no quantitative background. The design principle was to surface conclusions, not model outputs: each listing shows a mispricing status (overpriced, underpriced, or within band), the recommended adjustment in dollar terms, and an estimated occupancy impact, without exposing the underlying model mechanics.

  • Segment, market, and property type filters allow managers to focus on their assigned properties
  • Revenue impact is expressed as annualized dollars, not percentages, per manager feedback during testing
  • Demand score is shown as a percentile rank within submarket rather than as a raw score
  • Model version and last-refresh timestamp displayed on every view to maintain trust in data currency
The self-service dashboard eliminated a weekly reporting process where analysts prepared individual market summaries for manager review. Managers reported using the dashboard to make independent repricing decisions within the first month of deployment, which was the primary adoption milestone defined at engagement outset.

Engagement context
Automating Plain-Language Translation
of Quantitative Portfolio Reports

A financial services client produced monthly portfolio performance reports consumed primarily by non-quantitative leadership: board members, senior relationship managers, and operations staff without a modeling background. A quant analyst was spending 6 to 8 hours per monthly cycle manually translating model outputs and performance metrics into narrative prose, producing a document that leadership could read without interpreting raw numbers.

The client identified this translation step as both a bottleneck and a source of inconsistency, as narrative framing varied by analyst. The engagement objective was to automate this translation using the GPT-4 API while preserving factual accuracy, regulatory conservatism, and firm-specific terminology.

System design
Pipeline Architecture and
Prompt Engineering Approach

The pipeline accepts a structured Python dictionary of monthly metrics as input and returns a four-section narrative document: performance summary, attribution analysis, risk commentary, and forward outlook. Each section is generated in a separate API call to allow independent human review and to avoid context length constraints on the full document.

  • System prompt encodes firm-specific terminology, risk appetite language, and the set of claims that require explicit hedging
  • Temperature set to 0.2 for factual stability; tested at 0.0 and 0.4 with 0.2 chosen as the balance point between variance and naturalness
  • Chain-of-thought instruction in the prompt: model is asked to derive narrative from the data provided rather than from pattern-matched financial boilerplate
  • Explicit instruction to flag anomalies in the data rather than smooth over them in narrative

Prompt design
Engineering for Factual Accuracy
in a Regulated Context

Financial narrative is not a domain where generative fluency is the primary risk. The primary risks are factual inaccuracy (wrong numbers in narrative), regulatory overreach (unqualified forward-looking statements), and tone inconsistency. The prompt engineering effort was concentrated on controlling these failure modes rather than on stylistic quality.

The single highest-value prompt engineering decision was explicit instruction to flag input data anomalies rather than silently incorporate them into narrative. In the first production run, the model correctly surfaced a data entry error in the attribution table rather than generating a narrative that would have been factually wrong.
Key prompt constraints
  • All numerical claims must cite the specific input field they derive from (traceability requirement)
  • Forward-looking statements must be framed as conditional on stated assumptions, never as predictions
  • Regulatory trigger phrases ("guaranteed", "will", "expected to") are explicitly forbidden in the prompt
  • Any input metric outside two standard deviations of the prior 12-month range must be flagged for analyst review rather than incorporated silently
  • Firm-specific terminology glossary injected into system prompt; model instructed to use client terms only
Evaluation methodology
Measuring Output Quality
and Factual Fidelity

LLM outputs in financial contexts require a more rigorous evaluation framework than subjective quality assessment. A scoring rubric was developed with the client's compliance team covering four dimensions: numerical accuracy, regulatory language compliance, terminology adherence, and narrative completeness. Each section was scored against the rubric before the pipeline was approved for production use.

LLM Fine-Tuning versus Retrieval-Augmented Classification
Architecture evaluation  |  Document classification  |  LoRA, prompt-tuning, embedding retrieval
⌄
1 / 5
Evaluation objective
Selecting an LLM Architecture
for Internal Document Classification

A client with a large internal document corpus required an automated classification system capable of assigning documents to a predefined taxonomy of approximately 40 categories. Three candidate architectures were evaluated: LoRA fine-tuning of a 7B parameter base model, prompt-tuning via soft prefix injection, and embedding-based retrieval classification using a frozen encoder and a labeled reference index.

The evaluation framework prioritized classification accuracy on a held-out test set, total infrastructure cost over a 12-month horizon, operational complexity of the production deployment, and the architecture's ability to incorporate new document categories without retraining.

Recommended: embedding retrieval

Embedding retrieval produced the highest accuracy on this task while requiring substantially lower infrastructure investment. The result is counterintuitive relative to the common assumption that fine-tuning approaches should outperform retrieval on narrow classification tasks, and merits explanation.

The retrieval architecture outperformed LoRA primarily because the classification task is not stylistically novel: the 40 document categories are well-represented in the base model's pretraining data, so the embedding space already separates them effectively. Fine-tuning yields the largest gains on tasks requiring domain adaptation the base model has not seen; this task did not qualify.
Retrieval architecture details
Embedding Index Design
and Classification Logic

The reference index was built from a labeled set of 15 to 25 examples per category, embedded using text-embedding-3-small and stored in a Postgres table with the pgvector extension. At inference time, the query document is embedded and the top-5 nearest neighbors are retrieved by cosine similarity. Category assignment follows a weighted majority vote, with distance-weighted votes giving higher confidence to closer neighbors.

  • Index contains approximately 800 labeled examples across 40 categories (mean: 20 per category)
  • Rare categories with under 10 examples were augmented with lightly paraphrased variants; augmented examples are flagged and excluded from accuracy evaluation
  • Confidence threshold of 0.65 (weighted vote share) required for automatic classification; below-threshold documents routed to human review queue
  • New categories added by appending labeled examples to the index; no retraining or redeployment required
The zero-retraining property of the retrieval architecture was the decisive factor in the client recommendation. The document taxonomy evolves as regulatory requirements change; LoRA fine-tuning would require a retraining cycle and model validation for every taxonomy update. The retrieval approach required only adding examples to the index.

Recommendation rationale
Decision Framework and
Architectural Trade-offs
Embedding retrieval: recommended

The recommendation is based on four factors considered jointly: accuracy, cost, operational complexity, and adaptability to taxonomy change. Retrieval dominates on all four dimensions for this specific task.

The general conclusion should not be overgeneralized. LoRA fine-tuning remains the superior choice for tasks requiring stylistic adaptation (clinical note summarization, domain-specific code generation, specialized translation) where the base model's embedding space does not already separate the target categories. The evaluation outcome here is task-specific.
LoRA: not recommended for this task

Lower accuracy than retrieval despite substantially higher cost. GPU training infrastructure and model serving endpoint represent ongoing operational burden without compensating accuracy benefit on this particular classification taxonomy.

Prompt-tuning: not recommended

Lowest accuracy of the three approaches. The soft prefix approach did not produce meaningful task adaptation compared to zero-shot prompting, suggesting the 40-category taxonomy was either too broad for soft prompt capacity or that the labeled set was insufficient to learn a useful prefix representation.
